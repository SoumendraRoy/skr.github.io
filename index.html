Gravitational-wave astrophysics

Where do binary black hole mergers come from?

Gravitational-wave astronomer and Ph.D. candidate at Stony Brook University

Binary black holes merge throughout the Universe, but their origins remain uncertain. I use gravitational-wave observations to study how these systems form, evolve, and find their partners.

My research asks what the observed masses, spins, mass ratios, and cosmic history of binary black holes can tell us about the lives of massive stars and the environments in which black holes merge.

The central approach

Reading the black-hole population

Each gravitational-wave event provides only part of the story. The population as a whole preserves clues about the physical processes that created it.

01

Masses and pairing

Which black holes merge with one another?

I study structure in the two-dimensional mass distribution, including how binary pairing changes around the mid-thirties mass feature. These patterns can distinguish between isolated stellar evolution, dynamical formation, and hierarchical mergers.

02

Spins and formation history

How were the binaries assembled?

The magnitudes and orientations of black-hole spins contain information about stellar evolution, supernovae, and the environments in which binaries form. I use population-level spin measurements to test competing formation scenarios.

03

Mergers across cosmic time

When did these systems form?

The merger rate and black-hole population evolve with redshift. I study this evolution both to understand the history of compact-binary formation and to use gravitational waves as probes of cosmic expansion.

A clue in the mass spectrum

The mid-thirties mass feature

A particularly prominent feature appears near 35 solar masses. My work investigates whether this structure represents a distinct population of black holes and how its pairing behavior, spins, and redshift evolution constrain its astrophysical origin.

03

Beyond astrophysical origins

Gravitational-wave cosmology

Compact binaries also carry information about the Universe through which their waves travel. I develop population-informed and spectral-siren methods to measure the expansion history of the Universe using gravitational-wave observations.

About me

Connecting observations, formation, and cosmology

I am a Ph.D. candidate in gravitational-wave astrophysics at Stony Brook University, working with Professor Will M. Farr. I am also affiliated with the Center for Computational Astrophysics at the Flatiron Institute.

My research connects gravitational-wave observations, black-hole formation, statistical population inference, and cosmology.
